\subsection{Protocol}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Hardware:} Apple M4 Pro (14 cores), 48\,GB RAM, macOS 26.3, Apple clang 17 with
\texttt{-O3 -march=native}. Every run is single-threaded; eight runs execute in parallel, one per performance
core, so that wall-clock budgets stay comparable (the timing study of \cref{sec:complexity} ran alone).
\textbf{Budgets:} equal wall-clock time for GA, MA and TS --- 30\,s for $n\le50$, 60\,s otherwise, 1\,s for
the tiny instances; Clarke--Wright (CW) runs once (deterministic).
\textbf{Seeds:} 1--10 for evaluation, 101--105 for tuning (disjoint), all recorded in the result files.
\textbf{Parameters} (from the study in \cref{sec:sensitivity}): TS $\theta\in[10,25]$, $k=20$, $\delta=0.3$,
$\lambda_0=\overline{d(0,i)}/\bar q$, restart after $\max(2000,20n)$ idle iterations; GA $\mu=100$, $p_c=0.9$,
$p_m=1$, restart after 400 idle generations; MA $\mu=30$, $p_c=1$, $p_m=0.1$, $p_{ls}=0.5$, $k=20$.
\textbf{Validation:} all \nReported{} solutions reported by the \nRuns{} runs of this study were re-validated by the
independent Python checker (capacity, every customer exactly once, at most $K$ routes, cost recomputed from the
coordinates); none failed (\nNone{} runs found no fleet-feasible solution and reported none, see \cref{sec:beyond}). \textbf{Reference values:} the published BKS for CVRPLIB; for our own
instances the best solution found by any run of any experiment (20\,724.21 and 629.21\,km) --- eight additional
300\,s runs per instance found nothing better, and 15 of these 16 runs ended at exactly these values.

\Cref{tab:main} answers Task~7. \textbf{TS and MA solve every CVRPLIB instance to the BKS} --- in all 10 runs
on A-n32, A-n45 and A-n60; on A-n80-k10 MA reaches 1763 in 10/10 runs and TS in 6/10 (mean gap 0.03\,\%).
On the team instance TS is the most reliable (mean gap 0.006\,\%, 9 of 10 runs at the best value); on the real
Istanbul instance MA is slightly better (0.04\,\% vs 0.07\,\%). The pure \textbf{GA stays 1--5.5\,\% above} the
BKS and is the only stochastic method with large run-to-run variance (std up to 114 on the team instance).
CW is 3--9\,\% above and \emph{infeasible} for the fleet limit on A-n45-k6 and on the team instance.
A two-sided Mann--Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test confirms TS $\succ$ GA on every instance
($p\le2\cdot10^{-3}$, Vargha--Delaney $A_{12}\ge0.85$), whereas TS and MA are indistinguishable except on
A-n80-k10, where MA is better ($p=0.034$).

\paragraph{Convergence and anytime behaviour} \Cref{fig:convergence} shows \emph{how} the gaps arise. On A-n45-k6,
where the Clarke--Wright start violates the fleet limit, TS becomes feasible after about ten iterations ($<$1\,ms) and
is at the optimum almost immediately; MA starts from random tours and needs 0.1--1\,s, then overtakes TS on A-n80-k10.
The GA improves steadily but slowly. The attainment functions make the dispatcher's question concrete: on the
team instance, \emph{``a plan within 1\,\% in 1 second''} is achieved by every TS and MA run and by no GA run,
and the median TS run is within 0.1\,\% after 4.1\,s (all 10 runs within 7.6\,s) against 15.1\,s for MA (7 of 10 runs).

\paragraph{Routes and the strait} \Cref{fig:routes} visualises the best solutions. Of 17 drones on the team instance,
\textbf{10 cross the Bosphorus, each exactly twice}, and the European demand $q(E)=938$ needs at least
$\lceil938/100\rceil=10$ drones: the lower bound of \cref{prop:cross}(b) is \emph{attained} (the exact bin-packing
number is also 10). Nine of the ten crossing drones stay on the European side for the whole route; the tenth also
serves Asian clinics, which saves one vehicle compared with fully side-pure routing. All ten crossing drones fly 97--100\,\% full; as \cref{sec:bosphorus}
shows, this is caused by distance rather than by the penalty --- the European clusters are far from the laboratory, and
long out-and-back flights are only worth it with a full payload (the intuition behind the radial bound of
\cref{sec:approx}). The same structure appears on real geography: from the European depot, $q(A)=547$ forces at least
6 crossings, exactly 6 routes cross, and they are again 97--100\,\% loaded. On both instances not a single route
zig-zags (4 or more crossings).

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity}\label{sec:sensitivity}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{\figdir/sensitivity.pdf}
\caption{Parameter study (5 tuning seeds, 10\,s each). (a) TS mean gap for tenure range $\times$ granularity $k$
(team instance; frame = best cell). (b) The tenure effect on two instances (mean, min--max). (c) GA mean gap for
mutation rate $\times$ population size. (d) Mean broken-pairs distance of the GA population over time ($\mu=100$).}
\label{fig:sensitivity}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:sensitivity} shows a clear \emph{U-shape} for the tabu tenure: with $\theta\le3$ the search re-creates
the edges it just removed and cycles (gap 1.5\,\%), with $\theta\ge80$ too many edges are forbidden and good moves
are blocked (1.6--2.1\,\%); the valley $\theta\in[5,40]$ is wide, which makes TS robust. Granularity matters as much:
$k=5$ hides the moves needed to cross between clusters (gap $\ge1$\,\%), while $k\ge20$ suffices --- the granular
neighbourhood is $5\times$ cheaper than $k=n$ without losing quality. For the GA, the absence of mutation is the
only clearly bad setting (5.5\,\%): panel (d) shows why --- without mutation the population collapses to a broken-pairs
diversity of 0.1 within 50\,ms, whereas $p_m=1$ keeps it at 0.15--0.3 throughout. For MA, applying local search to
half of the offspring was best ($p_{ls}=0.5$: 0.16\,\%; $p_{ls}=0$: 4.0\,\%).

\subsection{Why TS and MA beat GA: search trajectories in partition space}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{\figdir/stn.pdf}
\caption{Search trajectory networks. (a) STN of the 30 runs on A-n45-k6: nodes are partitions of the clinics into
routes visited by the best-so-far trajectories, size $\propto$ visits. (b, c) The same trajectories in
``funnel'' coordinates: transfer distance to the best partition (customers that must change route) against the
gap. Horizontal steps change the clustering, vertical steps only the order inside routes.}
\label{fig:stn}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:stn} shows that the difference is \emph{structural}. On A-n45-k6 all ten TS and all ten MA trajectories end in
the same node --- the optimal partition --- while only 3 of 10 GA trajectories reach it; the other seven end in
separate sinks 4--11 relocations away. TS's first feasible solution is already only 2 relocations from the optimal
partition; everything after is sequencing (the vertical drop in panel b). On the team instance the 20 TS and MA runs
descend one shared funnel from 35 relocations to 0, whereas all GA runs stop 26--43 relocations away at 2--4\,\% gap.
The GA's operators act on the giant tour, where a good \emph{partition} is only implicit: a mutation that moves one
clinic to another route is a rare event among the $\Theta(n^2)$ possible changes of the permutation. TS and MA apply
exactly such relocations deliberately, $nk$ of them per step. \textbf{Partition-level moves, not population size, are
what the CVRP rewards} --- consistent with the success of hybrid genetic search \citep{vidal2012hybrid}.

\paragraph{A surprise: the Clarke--Wright seed is a double-edged sword} All ten GA runs on A-n32-k5 end at the
\emph{same} cost 827, and all ten on A-n80-k10 at 1818. The CW giant tour, recommended as a seed individual, is so much
better than random permutations that its descendants take over the population within milliseconds (a
``super-individual''), and partial restarts cannot compete with it. \input{sections/exp_ablation}
